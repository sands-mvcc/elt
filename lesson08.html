Oscillators, Timers & Counters

The circuits that give digital systems a heartbeat and a memory of time: oscillators generate the clock, timers shape precise intervals, and counters — built from Lesson 6's flip-flops — count edges into binary, BCD, up/down, and Johnson sequences.

⏱ 3 Hours Study 📋 9 Core Topics 📐 Symbol Library (6 circuits) 📈 555 Timer Scope + 4-Counter Lab

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain how an oscillator sustains a repeating waveform without an input signal, and distinguish RC relaxation oscillators from crystal oscillators by stability and application.
  • Analyze 555 timer astable operation — the capacitor cycling between ⅓ and ⅔ VCC — and calculate frequency with f = 1.44 / ((R₁+2R₂)C).
  • Calculate monostable (one-shot) pulse width using T = 1.1·R·C and identify applications for triggered timing pulses.
  • Describe how a quartz crystal plus a flip-flop divider chain produces precision clocks, from wristwatches to microprocessors.
  • Distinguish asynchronous (ripple) counters from synchronous counters, and explain modulus as the number of states a counter cycles through.
  • Trace a 4-bit binary counter through its 16 states, reading each output as a divide-by-2ⁿ frequency divider.
  • Explain how a BCD (decade) counter resets at ten, and how cascaded decades drive multi-digit displays through Lesson 7's decoders.
  • Analyze up/down counter direction control and Johnson (twisted-ring) counter operation, including its 2n-state sequence and glitch-free decoding.
  • Verify every circuit hands-on in the embedded 555 Timer Scope and Counter Lab, connecting oscillator → clock → counter into one working timing chain.

Timers

Definition
The most-manufactured IC in history — two comparators, a resistor divider setting ⅓ and ⅔ VCC thresholds, an SR latch (Lesson 6!), and a discharge transistor. Wire it two ways and it's an oscillator or a precision one-shot.
Monostable (One-Shot)
Timers

Definition
One stable state: the output rests LOW until triggered, produces exactly one pulse of width T = 1.1·R·C, then returns to rest. Used for debounce delays, precise strobe pulses, and timeout intervals.
Threshold & Trigger
Timers
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
The 555's two comparator inputs: Trigger fires the internal latch when the capacitor falls below ⅓ VCC; Threshold resets it when the capacitor rises above ⅔ VCC. The sawtooth bouncing between them is visible live in the Lab.
RC Time Constant (τ)
Timers
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
τ = R·C, the natural clock of every timing circuit: a capacitor charges to ~63% of its target in one τ. All 555 math is disguised time constants — bigger R or C, slower everything, in exact proportion.
Counters
Counter
Counters
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
A register of flip-flops that steps through a fixed sequence of states, one step per clock edge. Lesson 6's toggle behavior, organized: the outputs read as a number that increments with every pulse counted.
Modulus (MOD)
Counters
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
The number of unique states a counter cycles through before repeating. Four flip-flops give up to MOD-16 (0–15); a decade counter is MOD-10; a 4-bit Johnson counter is MOD-8. Modulus also equals the frequency division ratio.
Ripple (Asynchronous) Counter
Counters
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
Each flip-flop is clocked by the previous stage's output — the count "ripples" down the chain. Minimal wiring, but stage delays accumulate, so high-speed or glitch-sensitive designs use synchronous counters instead.
Synchronous Counter
Counters
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
Every flip-flop shares the same clock; gate logic decides which stages toggle each edge. All outputs change together — no ripple delay, no decoding glitches. The 74163 is the classic packaged example.
BCD (Decade) Counter
Counters
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
A MOD-10 counter: counts 0–9, then reset logic detects state 1010₂ and forces a return to 0000. One decade per decimal digit — cascade them and feed Lesson 7's BCD-to-seven-segment decoders to build any numeric display.
Up/Down Counter
Counters
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
A counter with a direction control: count up on one setting, down on the other (some versions use separate up/down clock inputs). The natural fit wherever a quantity both rises and falls — position, inventory, people in a room.
Johnson (Twisted-Ring) Counter
Counters
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
A shift register whose inverted last output feeds back to its input. n flip-flops give 2n states (4 FFs → 8 states) in a walking-bit pattern where only one bit changes per clock — so decoded outputs are glitch-free.
Terminal Count / Carry Out
Counters
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
The output that pulses when a counter reaches its final state (15 for MOD-16, 9 for a decade). It clocks the next counter in a cascade — how single digits become odometers, frequency counters, and digital clocks.

Core Lesson Content

Giving Digital Systems a Heartbeat

Lessons 6 and 7 kept saying "on the clock edge" — but where do clock edges come from? This capstone lesson completes the picture with the timing chain every digital system contains:

  1. Oscillators generate the repeating waveform — the raw heartbeat — with no input signal at all.
  2. Timers shape precise intervals from that behavior: a pulse exactly this wide, a delay exactly that long.
  3. Counters — flip-flops from Lesson 6, organized — count the edges into numbers, divide frequencies, and sequence events.
Oscillator → clock edges → counters count them → decoders (L7) display them
A digital clock, a frequency counter, and a stopwatch are all this one chain.
💡 The Whole Course in One Circuit
A 555 timer is a resistor-capacitor charging circuit (Lesson 2's time constants), watched by two comparators (Lesson 4), driving an SR latch (Lesson 6), whose output clocks counters (this lesson) that feed display decoders (Lesson 7). Every device on the chip's die is something you've already mastered — this lesson just connects them into time.

Oscillators: Output Without Input

Every amplifier in Lesson 4 needed an input signal. An oscillator needs none — it manufactures its own repeating waveform from two ingredients:

  • Positive feedback: a portion of the output returned in-phase to the input, reinforcing any change until the circuit swings rail to rail (the same positive feedback that made Lesson 4's Schmitt trigger snap).
  • A timing element: something that delays the swing-back — an RC network, an LC tank, or a quartz crystal — setting the period of the resulting oscillation.

The Relaxation Oscillator

The workhorse pattern for digital clocks: a capacitor charges toward a threshold, a comparator snaps the output, the capacitor discharges toward a second threshold, and the comparator snaps back. Charge, snap, discharge, snap — forever. The output is a square wave (perfect for clocking logic) and the capacitor voltage is a sawtooth bouncing between the two thresholds — both waveforms are drawn live in the Lab's 555 mode.

The 555 Timer in Astable Mode

The 555 — billions manufactured yearly since 1972 — packages the relaxation oscillator into eight pins. Inside: a resistor divider creating ⅓ and ⅔ VCC reference points, two comparators watching them, an SR latch, and a discharge transistor. In astable wiring, external parts R₁, R₂, and C set the tempo:

  1. C charges toward VCC through R₁ + R₂; output is HIGH.
  2. C reaches ⅔ VCC → threshold comparator resets the latch → output LOW, discharge transistor opens a path.
  3. C discharges through R₂ only toward ground.
  4. C falls to ⅓ VCC → trigger comparator sets the latch → output HIGH — back to step 1, forever.
f = 1.44 / ((R₁ + 2R₂)·C)   ·   Duty = (R₁+R₂)/(R₁+2R₂)
Example: R₁=10 kΩ, R₂=47 kΩ, C=10 µF → f = 1.44/(104k × 10µ) ≈ 1.4 Hz, duty ≈ 55%
⚠️ Watch Out: Duty Cycle Is Always Above 50%
Charging runs through two resistors, discharging through one — so HIGH time always exceeds LOW time in the basic circuit. Need a true 50% square wave? Make R₂ ≫ R₁ (approaches 50%), add a diode across R₂, or simply divide the output by 2 with a T flip-flop — Lesson 6's trick, which delivers a perfect 50% regardless.

Applications

LED flashers and tone generators, clock sources for counters where a few percent of drift is fine, PWM foundations, and — historically — half the hobby-electronics projects ever published.

The 555 Timer in Monostable Mode: The One-Shot

Rewire the same chip and it becomes a precision pulse-maker with one stable state: output LOW, capacitor held empty. Then:

  1. A falling edge on the trigger input (below ⅓ VCC) sets the latch — output snaps HIGH.
  2. C charges through R toward VCC.
  3. When C reaches ⅔ VCC, the threshold comparator resets the latch — output returns LOW and C is discharged, ready for the next trigger.
Pulse width:  T = 1.1 · R · C   (time to charge from 0 to ⅔ VCC ≈ 1.1 time constants)
Example: R = 100 kΩ, C = 10 µF → T = 1.1 × 1.0 s = 1.1 seconds — regardless of how long the trigger lasts

That last clause is the magic: a sloppy, bouncing, variable-length input produces one clean, exactly-timed output pulse. The one-shot is a pulse standardizer.

Applications

  • Switch debouncing: a bouncing pushbutton (dozens of edges) becomes one clean pulse — solving the very problem Lesson 6's SR latch debouncer addressed, with adjustable timing.
  • Timed intervals: darkroom timers, delay-off lighting, watchdog timeouts, missing-pulse detectors.
  • Pulse-width standardization ahead of counters — guaranteeing every counted event has identical width.
🎯 Quiz Tip
Two formulas carry this chip: astable f = 1.44/((R₁+2R₂)C), monostable T = 1.1RC. Anchor them by remembering which mode has two resistors — the oscillator charges through two, the one-shot through one.

Crystal Oscillators and System Clocks

RC oscillators drift — resistor tolerance, capacitor aging, and temperature swing the 555's frequency by a few percent. Fine for a blinking LED; catastrophic for a watch (a 1% error is ~14 minutes per day). Precision timing uses quartz:

  • The piezoelectric bargain: squeeze quartz and it makes a voltage; apply a voltage and it flexes. Cut a crystal to size and it rings at one mechanical resonant frequency with extraordinary purity — an electrically-accessible tuning fork.
  • In the feedback loop: replace the RC timing network with the crystal and the oscillator locks to the crystal's resonance — stable to parts per million (seconds per month, not minutes per day).
  • The magic number 32,768: watch crystals are cut for 32,768 Hz = 2¹⁵ Hz, so a chain of fifteen T flip-flops (Lesson 6's divide-by-2, fifteen times) delivers exactly 1 Hz. Every quartz watch on Earth is this circuit.
32,768 Hz → ÷2¹⁵ (fifteen toggle stages) → 1.000 Hz → drives the seconds counter

Clock Distribution in Real Systems

A microprocessor board typically runs one crystal oscillator, then uses dividers (counters!) and multipliers to derive every internal clock — CPU, memory, peripherals — from that single disciplined source. When a technician sees timing chaos across a whole board, the crystal and its oscillator circuit are the first suspects: one part, every symptom.

💡 Design Trade Summary
555/RC: cheap, adjustable, drifty — use where "about right" is right. Crystal: fixed-frequency, ppm-stable — use wherever time is money, data, or law (clocks, communications, instrumentation).

Counter Fundamentals

A counter is Lesson 6's payoff: flip-flops arranged so their collective state steps through a fixed sequence, one step per clock edge. Read the outputs as a binary number (Lesson 5) and the circuit literally counts the pulses it has received.

Modulus: A Counter's Size

MOD = number of unique states before the sequence repeats
n flip-flops → up to 2ⁿ states  ·  4 FFs → MOD-16 (counts 0–15)  ·  MOD also = frequency division ratio

The ripple counter's charm is its honesty: it's nothing but the T flip-flop divide-by-2 from Lesson 6, chained. Q0 toggles every clock (÷2), Q1 toggles every Q0 cycle (÷4), Q2 (÷8), Q3 (÷16) — and read together, Q3Q2Q1Q0 is the count in binary. Counting and frequency division are the same circuit viewed two ways.

The 4-Bit Binary Counter and the BCD (Decade) Counter

4-Bit Binary: MOD-16

Four flip-flops count 0000 through 1111 — decimal 0 to 15 — then roll over to 0000 and raise a terminal count / carry out pulse. That carry clocks the next 4-bit counter in a cascade, exactly as a car odometer's units wheel nudges the tens wheel. Applications: event counting, address generation, timers, and ÷16 prescalers.

BCD (Decade): MOD-10

Humans read decimal, so display systems count in binary-coded decimal: each digit gets its own MOD-10 counter that counts 0–9 and resets. The construction trick:

Count normally 0000→1001 (0–9). The moment state 1010₂ (10) appears,
a NAND gate watching Q3·Q1 fires the asynchronous CLEAR → counter snaps to 0000.
  • The reset is Lesson 5 logic: 1010 is the first state where Q3 AND Q1 are both high — two gate inputs detect it. The illegal state exists for only nanoseconds.
  • Cascading decades: each counter's rollover clocks the next digit. Three decades + three of Lesson 7's BCD-to-seven-segment decoders = a 0–999 display. This is the anatomy of every digital multimeter, frequency counter, and scoreboard.
  • Division by ten: a decade counter is also a ÷10 prescaler — chain them for ÷100, ÷1000 in instrumentation.
💡 Watch the Reset Happen
In the Lab, select BCD and single-step the clock past 9. The count snaps to 0 — and the decimal readout confirms the counter never displays 10 through 15. Compare against Binary mode, which sails on to 15 before rolling over.

The Up/Down Counter

Some quantities only grow — elapsed seconds, total visitors. Others rise and fall — items in stock, position on a track, people in a room. The up/down counter serves the second kind with a direction control:

  • Direction input: one control line selects the sequence — UP counts 0,1,2,…,15,0 while DOWN counts 15,14,…,0,15. Internally, gating chooses whether each stage watches the previous stage's Q (up) or Q̅ (down).
  • Dual-clock versions: some parts (the classic 74193) instead provide separate count-up and count-down clock inputs — pulse whichever event occurred.
  • Borrow and carry: full up/down parts provide both a carry (rolled over the top) and a borrow (rolled under zero) for cascading in either direction.

Applications

  • Position tracking: a motor encoder pulses up for one direction of rotation, down for the other — the count is the position. CNC machines and 3D printers live on this.
  • Inventory and occupancy: entry sensor counts up, exit sensor counts down — parking garages ("SPACES: 47") and room-occupancy limits.
  • Elevator floor indicators, kitchen-timer countdowns, and any set-then-count-to-zero interval logic.
🎯 Lab Check
In Counter mode, select Up/Down, count up to 5, flip the direction button, and step again: 4, 3, 2… Watch the timing lanes reverse their pattern — Q0 still toggles every edge, but the higher bits now step down through the codes.

The Johnson (Twisted-Ring) Counter — and the Family Portrait

Construction: One Twist

Take Lesson 6's shift register and close the loop — feed the last output back to the input and data circulates in a ring. Now add the twist: feed back the inverted last output (Q̅₃ → D₀). That single inversion creates the Johnson counter:

4 flip-flops, sequence (Q3Q2Q1Q0): 0000 → 0001 → 0011 → 0111 → 1111 → 1110 → 1100 → 1000 → 0000 …
A wave of 1s washes in, then washes out — 2n states from n flip-flops (here MOD-8)

Why Designers Love It

  • Only one bit changes per clock. Decode any state with a single 2-input AND gate, and the decoded outputs are glitch-free — no ripple-counter race conditions, no momentary false states.
  • 2n states beats the plain ring counter's n states for the same hardware (a 4-FF ring gives only 4 states; the twist doubles it).
  • Self-clocking sequencing: the overlapping waveforms are naturally phase-shifted square waves — historically used for quadrature signals and switch-tail sequencers.

Applications: state sequencers and phase generators (stepper-motor drive waveforms), the classic 4017-style LED chasers (a Johnson counter plus decoding), and glitch-free timing-signal generation.
