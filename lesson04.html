Transistor Amplifiers & Op-Amps

Small-signal AC amplifiers, voltage gain and impedance analysis for common-emitter circuits, and an introduction to op-amp inverting and non-inverting configurations — gain equations, virtual ground, and feedback fundamentals.

⏱ 3 Hours Study 📋 9 Core Topics 📐 Circuit Illustration Library 📈 6-Config Op-Amp Grapher 🧪 6 Companion Simulators

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain how a common-emitter amplifier superimposes a small AC signal on the DC bias from Lesson 3, and the role of coupling and bypass capacitors.
  • Calculate common-emitter voltage gain using AV ≈ −RC/r'e, where r'e = 25 mV / IE.
  • Analyze input and output impedance of a CE stage and predict loading effects when stages are cascaded.
  • Identify the op-amp symbol, its differential inputs, and the ideal characteristics: infinite gain, infinite input impedance, zero output impedance.
  • Apply the two golden rules of negative feedback — no input current, zero differential voltage — to analyze any op-amp circuit.
  • Derive and use the gain equations for the inverting (AV = −Rf/Rin) and non-inverting (AV = 1 + Rf/Rin) configurations, including the concept of virtual ground.
  • Describe the voltage follower and comparator, and explain why one uses full negative feedback while the other uses none.
  • Predict the output waveforms of integrator and differentiator circuits for square, triangle, and sine inputs.
  • Verify every configuration hands-on using the embedded waveform grapher and the six companion circuit simulators.

Definition
Returning a portion of the output to the inverting input, opposing the input change. It trades raw gain for precision: stable, resistor-ratio-defined gain, wider bandwidth, and lower distortion.

The actual circuit gain with feedback applied — set by external resistors, not the op-amp. Because AOL is enormous, ACL depends almost only on the feedback network: swap resistors, change the gain.

Core Lesson Content

From Holding a Point to Moving a Signal

Lesson 3 ended with a transistor parked at a Q-point — a DC state. Amplification is what happens when you let a small AC signal wiggle the operating point around that Q: a few millivolts at the base become volts at the collector. This lesson follows that idea up the abstraction ladder:

  1. The discrete stage: a single common-emitter amplifier — how gain arises, what sets it, and why impedance matters when stages connect.
  2. The integrated leap: the operational amplifier — dozens of matched transistors packaged as one near-ideal gain block, tamed by feedback.
  3. The six classic configurations every technician meets: inverting, non-inverting, follower, comparator, integrator, differentiator.
💡 Why Both Halves Matter to a Technician
Op-amps have replaced discrete amplifiers in most designs — but the CE stage is still inside every op-amp, output driver, and RF front end you'll service. Learn the CE stage to understand what you're probing; learn the op-amp to understand 90% of the analog boards on your bench.

The Common-Emitter Small-Signal Amplifier

Take the voltage-divider-biased CE circuit from Lesson 3 and add three capacitors — that's the complete classic amplifier:

  • Input coupling capacitor: passes the AC signal into the base while blocking the source from disturbing the DC bias.
  • Output coupling capacitor: delivers only the amplified AC to the load, stripping off the collector's DC level.
  • Emitter bypass capacitor: for DC it's invisible (the emitter resistor still stabilizes the Q-point); for AC it's a short circuit, grounding the emitter so the full signal appears across the base-emitter junction — maximizing gain.

The Signal's Journey

A small positive swing at the base increases VBE, which increases IC (exponentially sensitive — Lesson 1's diode curve at work), which increases the drop across RC, which pulls the collector voltage down. Input up → output down: the CE stage inherently inverts (180° phase shift), exactly like the inverting op-amp configuration you'll meet in Tab 5.

⚠️ Watch Out: The Signal Must Stay Small
"Small-signal" is a real constraint, not a name. Swing the base too far and the operating point leaves the linear region: the output flattens against saturation on one side and cutoff on the other — clipping. On a scope, clipped audio looks like a sine wave with a haircut.
🎯 Bench Check
Probing a healthy CE stage: DC-couple the scope and you'll see the small AC signal riding on the DC collector voltage (the Q-point from Lesson 3). AC-couple to measure just the signal — the same coupling trick the circuit itself uses.

Voltage Gain and Impedance Analysis

Where CE Gain Comes From

For AC, the transistor's emitter presents a tiny internal resistance:

r'e ≈ 25 mV / IE   →   AV ≈ −RC / r'e  (bypassed emitter)

Worked example: a CE stage biased at IE = 1 mA with RC = 3.6 kΩ: r'e = 25 mV / 1 mA = 25 Ω, so AV ≈ −3600/25 = −144. A 10 mV input becomes a 1.44 V inverted output. Notice the gain rides on IE — which drifts with temperature — one of several reasons designers migrated to op-amps.

With the bypass capacitor removed, the emitter resistor enters the AC path: AV ≈ −RC/(r'e + RE) — much lower gain, but far more stable and predictable. Gain traded for stability: your first taste of feedback (RE is feedback).

Impedance: Why Stages Load Each Other

PropertyTypical CE StageConsequence
Input impedance≈1–5 kΩ (bias divider ∥ β·r'e)Loads down high-impedance sources — a weak sensor loses most of its signal at the door
Output impedance≈RC (kΩ range)Gain collapses when a heavy (low-Z) load is connected: the load parallels RC

Cascade two CE stages and the second stage's input impedance loads the first stage's output — realized gain is always less than the product of the unloaded gains. Managing this loading is half of discrete amplifier design, and it's precisely the problem op-amps erase: near-infinite input Z, near-zero output Z.

🎯 Quiz Tip
Two formulas answer most CE questions: r'e = 25 mV/IE, then AV = −RC/r'e. Half the trick is remembering that doubling the bias current halves r'e and therefore doubles the gain.

Meet the Op-Amp

An operational amplifier is a complete multi-stage amplifier on one chip — a differential input pair, gain stages, and a push-pull output driver. From outside, it's five pins that matter: two inputs, one output, two supply rails. It amplifies the difference between its inputs:

Vout = AOL × (V₊ − V₋)   with AOL ≈ 100,000+

The Ideal Op-Amp (and how close reality gets)

PropertyIdealReal (LM741 / LM358 class)
Open-loop gainInfinite100,000 – 1,000,000
Input impedanceInfinite (no input current)MΩ–TΩ (nA of bias current)
Output impedanceZeroTens of ohms
Output swingUnlimitedWithin 1–2 V of the rails (±Vsat)
SpeedInstantSlew-rate limited (0.5 V/µs for a 741)

A gain of 100,000 sounds wonderful and is nearly useless raw: a 150 µV difference between the inputs already slams the output into a rail. The insight that makes op-amps practical is to throw most of that gain away deliberately through negative feedback — covered in the next two tabs and formalized in Tab 9.

💡 The Two Golden Rules
When negative feedback is present, analyze ANY op-amp circuit with two statements: (1) The inputs draw no current. (2) The output does whatever it takes to make V₋ equal V₊. Every gain equation in this lesson is these two rules plus Ohm's law.

The Inverting Amplifier and Virtual Ground

Ground the + input. Feed the signal through Rin to the − input, and connect Rf from the output back to the − input. Now apply the golden rules:

  1. Rule 2: the output drives V₋ to equal V₊ = 0 V. The − input sits at ground potential without being connected to ground — the virtual ground.
  2. Input current is therefore I = Vin/Rin (the full input voltage appears across Rin).
  3. Rule 1: none of that current can enter the op-amp — it must all continue through Rf, forcing Vout = −I·Rf.
AV = −Rf / Rin   (e.g., Rf=100 kΩ, Rin=10 kΩ → gain = −10)

Everything a technician needs is in that equation: gain is set by two resistors you can read off the board, the minus sign means the output is inverted, and the input impedance is simply Rin (the source looks into a resistor that ends at a virtual ground).

⚠️ Watch Out
Because input impedance = Rin, an inverting amp with a small Rin can load a weak source badly — the same loading problem from Tab 3. High-impedance sources belong on the non-inverting configuration instead.

Non-Inverting Amplifier and the Voltage Follower

Non-Inverting: Gain Without Loading

Drive the + input directly with the signal. Rf and Rin form a voltage divider from the output to ground, with its midpoint feeding the − input. Golden rule 2 forces that midpoint to equal Vin, so the output must rise to:

AV = 1 + Rf / Rin   (e.g., Rf=90 kΩ, Rin=10 kΩ → gain = +10, no inversion)
  • Input impedance is essentially infinite — the signal touches only the op-amp's + input, which draws no current. The perfect home for high-impedance sensors.
  • Minimum gain is 1: with the divider in place the output can never be smaller than the input. Need attenuation with inversion? That's the inverting amp's job (Rf < Rin).

The Voltage Follower: Gain of Exactly One, On Purpose

Delete both resistors and wire the output straight to the − input: 100% feedback, AV = 1. Why build an amplifier that doesn't amplify? Impedance transformation. Enormous input Z, near-zero output Z — it's Lesson 3's emitter follower perfected. Place it between a weak source (pH probe, high-value divider, sample-and-hold capacitor) and anything that needs current.

🎯 Quiz Tip
Same resistors, different answers: Rf = Rin gives gain −1 in the inverting configuration but +2 in the non-inverting. If an exam answer is off by "one plus," check which configuration the schematic shows — where does the signal enter?

The Comparator: An Op-Amp With No Leash

Remove all feedback and the op-amp's full open-loop gain runs wild — which is exactly what you want for making decisions. With a reference voltage on one input and a signal on the other:

V₊ > V₋ → Vout = +Vsat   |   V₊ < V₋ → Vout = −Vsat

The output lives at the rails, snapping between them as the signal crosses the reference. Analog in, digital-style decision out — the bridge between this course's analog half and its digital half.

Where You'll Meet Comparators

  • Threshold alarms: over-temperature, low-battery, light/dark switches (a thermistor or photodiode from Lesson 2 sets one input).
  • Zero-crossing detectors: firing the TRIAC phase-control circuits from Lesson 3 in sync with the AC line.
  • Waveform squaring: turning any periodic signal into a clean logic-compatible square wave.
⚠️ Watch Out: Chatter and the Schmitt Fix
A noisy signal drifting slowly through the threshold crosses it many times — the output chatters rail-to-rail. The cure is a little positive feedback (a resistor from output to the + input), creating two separated thresholds — hysteresis, the Schmitt trigger. Watch for it in the grapher's comparator mode with noise enabled.

The Integrator and the Differentiator

Swap one resistor of the inverting amplifier for a capacitor and the circuit stops multiplying and starts doing calculus — in real time, on real voltages.

Integrator: Capacitor in the Feedback Path

Vout = −(1/RinCf) ∫ Vin dt

The virtual ground still fixes the input current at Vin/Rin; that current now charges Cf, so the output ramps at a rate proportional to the input level. Consequences you can see on a scope:

  • Square wave in → triangle wave out (constant input = constant-slope ramp, flipping with each half-cycle).
  • DC in → runaway ramp — the circuit integrates forever until it hits a rail, which is why practical integrators add a large bleed resistor across Cf.
  • Applications: ramp/sweep generators, triangle-wave oscillators, active filters, and the front half of analog PID controllers.

Differentiator: Capacitor at the Input

Vout = −RfCin × dVin/dt

Current only flows through an input capacitor when the input is changing, so the output reports the input's slope:

  • Triangle in → square out (constant slopes become constant levels).
  • Square in → sharp spikes at each edge — instant edge detection.
  • Applications: rate-of-change alarms (how fast is the temperature rising?), edge detectors, FM demodulation. Practical versions add a small series resistor to tame high-frequency noise, which differentiation amplifies aggressively.
💡 The Symmetry Worth Remembering
Integrator and differentiator are the same inverting skeleton with the capacitor on opposite sides — and their behaviors are exact inverses: one turns squares into triangles, the other turns triangles back into squares. Prove it in the grapher: run square → integrator, then feed a triangle to the differentiator.

Feedback Fundamentals: The Big Idea

Every circuit in this lesson is one idea wearing six costumes: take an absurd amount of raw gain and spend it buying precision.

What Negative Feedback Buys

  • Gain set by parts you choose: AOL varies chip-to-chip and with temperature; Rf/Rin is a ratio of 1% resistors. Closed-loop gain inherits the resistors' precision, not the chip's chaos.
  • Bandwidth: gain × bandwidth ≈ constant (the GBP, ~1 MHz for a 741). Configure less gain, receive more bandwidth — a trade you control.
  • Lower distortion, stable impedances: feedback continuously corrects the output against the input, flattening nonlinearities that plague open-loop stages like the CE amplifier.
