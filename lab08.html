Counters: From Toggling Flip-Flops to a Digital Readout

The Flip-Flop lab ended with a prediction — chain toggling flip-flops and you've built a binary counter. This lab makes you keep that promise: build a ripple counter from 74HC74s, graduate to the 74HC193 synchronous up/down counter, bend its modulus to decimal with one AND gate, and finish with the payoff every digital course builds toward — a counter driving a CD4511 decoder driving a real 7-segment display, counting 0 through 9 on its own.

⏱ 2 × 75-Minute Sessions 🖥 Tinkercad Circuits (Free) 🔧 '74 Ripple · '193 Up/Down · 4511 + Display 📊 Live 7-Segment Counter Playground ✅ 6-Question Knowledge Check

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lab, you will be able to:

  • Construct a 2-bit asynchronous (ripple) counter from 74HC74 flip-flops in toggle configuration (Q̄ → D, each Q clocking the next stage), and verify the binary count sequence 00 → 01 → 10 → 11.
  • Explain why each ripple stage divides frequency by two, and identify the ripple counter's weakness: clock edges propagate stage-by-stage, so outputs change at slightly different times.
  • Operate the 74HC193 synchronous 4-bit up/down counter: wire its dual clock inputs, active-HIGH CLR, active-LOW LOAD̄, and parallel data pins correctly, and contrast synchronous counting (all bits clocked together) with ripple counting.
  • Demonstrate up-counting (0→15) and down-counting (15→0) on the same chip, and interpret the CARRȲ and BORROW̄ outputs that make multi-digit cascading possible.
  • Modify the counter's modulus: design and wire a detect-and-clear circuit (QD·QB → 74HC08 → CLR) that converts the mod-16 '193 into a mod-10 decade counter, and generalize the method to any modulus.
  • Integrate a complete digital counting instrument — '193 decade counter → CD4511 BCD decoder/driver → 7-segment display — and trace a single clock pulse through count, code, and glowing digit.
  • Calculate frequency division through counter chains (fout = fclock/modulus), and describe how cascading decade stages via the carry output extends the display to 0–99 and beyond.

Key Terms & Concepts

Click any card to reveal its definition. Review these before you build.

Counter Fundamentals
Binary Counter
Counter Fundamentals
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
A chain of toggling flip-flops whose outputs, read together, count clock pulses in binary. Each stage runs at half the rate of the one before — bit 0 flips every pulse, bit 1 every second pulse, bit 2 every fourth. Counting and frequency division are the same circuit viewed two ways.
Ripple (Asynchronous) Counter
Counter Fundamentals
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
The simplest counter: only stage 0 sees the real clock; each Q output clocks the next stage. Cheap and elegant — but the count "ripples" through the chain, each stage a propagation delay late. During that ripple the outputs briefly show wrong codes: harmless for LEDs, hazardous for decoders.
Synchronous Counter
Counter Fundamentals
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
Every flip-flop receives the same clock edge simultaneously; internal logic decides which bits toggle. All outputs change together — no ripple, no transient false codes. The 74HC193 is this architecture, which is why it can safely drive a decoder. Nearly all counters inside CPUs are synchronous.
Modulus (MOD)
Counter Fundamentals
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
The number of distinct states a counter visits before repeating. Four flip-flops give a natural MOD-16 (0–15); a decade counter is MOD-10 (0–9). The modulus is also the frequency-division ratio: a MOD-10 counter's top bit cycles at fclock/10. Your digital clock is MOD-60 and MOD-24 counters holding hands.
The 74HC193
Dual Clock Inputs (UP / DOWN)
The 74HC193
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
The '193's direction scheme: pulse pin 5 to count UP, pin 4 to count DOWN — the idle clock pin must be held HIGH. Direction is chosen by which pin you pulse, not by a mode switch (its cousin the 74HC191 uses a single clock + direction pin instead).
CLR — Active HIGH ⚠
The 74HC193
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
The '193's famous exception: unlike almost every 74xx control pin you've met, its clear (pin 14) is active HIGH — tie it LOW for normal counting, raise it to force 0000. The 7402-pinout lesson returns: read the datasheet, not the habit. In Part 4 this "quirk" becomes a gift: your detect gate drives CLR directly, no inverter needed.
Parallel LOAD̄
The 74HC193
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
Pull pin 11 LOW and the counter instantly adopts whatever value sits on data pins A–D (15, 1, 10, 9) — asynchronously, no clock needed. Tie it HIGH to count normally. LOAD̄ is how counters start from arbitrary values: timers that count down from 25, addresses that begin mid-memory.
CARRȲ & BORROW̄
The 74HC193
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
Cascade outputs: CARRȲ (pin 12) pulses LOW as the counter rolls over the top (15→0 counting up); BORROW̄ (pin 13) pulses LOW rolling under (0→15 counting down). Wire CARRȲ to the next stage's UP clock and the second digit advances once per full cycle of the first — exactly how 0–99 displays are built.
The Display System
Decade (BCD) Counter
The Display System
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
A MOD-10 counter cycling 0000–1001: the natural partner for decimal displays, because its output is always a valid BCD code the 4511 can decode. You'll manufacture one from the MOD-16 '193 with a detect-and-clear gate — the same trick that builds MOD-anything.
Detect-and-Clear
The Display System
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
The modulus-bending method: an AND gate watches for the first unwanted count and fires the clear. For MOD-10, the first illegal state is 10 = binary 1010 — and since no earlier count has QD and QB high together, detecting just those two bits suffices: QD·QB → CLR. The state 10 exists for nanoseconds, then vanishes.
Decoder/Driver (CD4511)
The Display System
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
Your Interface-lab decoder, now in its natural habitat: BCD in (pins 7, 1, 2, 6), seven segment-drive outputs out. "Driver" is earned — its outputs source enough current to light real segments through the 220 Ω resistors. Classic TTL twin: 7447 (active-LOW, common-anode displays).
Cascading
The Display System
🔄 Click to reveal definition
Definition
Chaining counter stages so each counts the previous stage's rollovers: ones digit → tens digit → hundreds. Each decade stage divides frequency by 10 and adds a display digit. Every odometer, scoreboard, and frequency counter is this pattern repeated — and your Part 5 circuit is its first link.

🖥 Tinkercad Setup Notes for Counter Systems

Eighth lab — every chip is a returning cast member, which is the point:

  • 74HC74 (Flip-Flop lab): toggle configuration is one wire — Q̄ (pin 6) back to D (pin 2) — and the flip-flop flips on every clock edge. Two of them, Q of the first clocking the second, is Part 1's ripple counter. Remember: PRE̅ and CLR̄ (pins 4, 1 / 10, 13) tied HIGH or the counter free-associates.
  • 74HC193 (cameo in the Interface lab, star here): 16-pin. The three pins that bite: CLR pin 14 is active HIGH — tie LOW to count; LOAD̄ pin 11 — tie HIGH; and the idle clock pin must sit HIGH (counting up → pin 4 to +5 V; counting down → pin 5 to +5 V). Outputs QA–QD on pins 3, 2, 6, 7.
  • CD4511 + 7-segment (Interface lab): same wiring as before — BCD in on scattered pins A=7, B=1, C=2, D=6; controls LT̄(3)/BL̄(4) HIGH, LE(5) LOW; one 220 Ω per segment; display common cathode → GND. This time the BCD source is the counter, not switches.
  • Clocks: pushbutton + 10 kΩ pull-down for single-step debugging (Tinkercad's buttons don't bounce; real ones would double-count — the Flip-Flop lab's debounce note applies doubly to counters). Function generator square, 1–2 Hz, 0–5 V for free-running counts. Always single-step a new build before free-running it.
  • Watching bits: four LED + 220 Ω indicators on QA–QD. Arrange them MSB on the left so the LEDs read as a binary number — a small habit that prevents endless transcription errors.
The '193's Three Tie-Downs
Ninety percent of dead-counter reports trace to one of three pins: CLR floating or HIGH (chip held at zero — and it's active-HIGH, the opposite of your reflex), LOAD̄ floating or LOW (chip endlessly reloading the data pins), or the idle clock pin left floating instead of HIGH (erratic or no counting). Wire pins 14, 11, and the unused clock before anything else, every time you seat a '193.

📐 The Circuits You Will Build

Four figures, one ascent: flip-flops that count by accident, a chip that counts on purpose, a gate that teaches it decimal, and a display that shows the world.

Figure 1 — 2-bit ripple counter: the Flip-Flop lab's prediction, built. Each '74 flip-flop toggles because its own Q̄ feeds its D (red wires). Only FF #1 sees your clock; FF #2 is clocked by Q0 itself (green) — the count ripples down the chain rather than arriving everywhere at once. Two chips would extend this to 4 bits; the '193 in the next tab does it better.
Figure 2 — The 74HC193 and its three tie-downs. Note everything that breaks habit: outputs are scattered (QA=3, QB=2, QC=6, QD=7), there are TWO clocks (pulse one, park the other HIGH), and CLR is active HIGH — the opposite of the '74's CLR̄ you just used. Data inputs A–D (15, 1, 10, 9) feed the LOAD̄ function; ground them when unused for tidiness.
Figure 3 — Bending MOD-16 to MOD-10. The AND gate watches QD and QB. Counts 0–9 never have both high; count 10 (1010) is the first that does — the gate output jumps HIGH and, because the '193's clear is active HIGH, drives pin 14 directly. The counter resets before your eye (or the 4511) ever sees a 10. Swap which bits you detect and the same trick makes MOD-anything.
Figure 4 — The capstone: a self-counting digital display. Clock → MOD-10 counter → BCD decoder/driver → glowing digit, 0 through 9, forever. Every wire in it is one you've placed before; the achievement is the system. The dashed path shows the one-wire upgrade to 0–99: carry feeds the next stage's clock, decades all the way up — the anatomy of every scoreboard and odometer.

🛠 Step-by-Step Procedure

Session 1 = Parts 1–3 (ripple + '193 up/down). Session 2 = Parts 4–5 (decade + display) + analysis. Single-step every new build with the button before free-running it.

Part 1 — Ripple Counter from Flip-Flops (≈25 min)

  1. Workspace. New circuit "LastName – Counter Lab." Breadboard, 5.00 V to rails. Pushbutton clock (button from +5 V, 10 kΩ pull-down) and two LED + 220 Ω indicators, placed Q1 left, Q0 right.
  2. Seat the 74HC74 (14 → +5 V, 7 → GND) and tie all four PRE̅/CLR̄ pins HIGH: 1, 4, 10, 13.
  3. Make both flip-flops toggle. FF #1: wire Q̄ (pin 6) → D (pin 2). FF #2: Q̄ (pin 8) → D (pin 12). One wire each — the Flip-Flop lab's T configuration.
  4. Chain them. Button → FF #1 clock (pin 3). Q0 (pin 5) → FF #2 clock (pin 11) — the ripple connection. Q0 → right LED, Q1 (pin 9) → left LED.
  5. Count by hand. Press the button repeatedly and read the LEDs as a 2-bit number after each press. Record eight presses in Data Table 1: 00 → 01 → 10 → 11 → 00 … Two things to notice and note: Q0 flips every press; Q1 flips only when Q0 falls (edge direction matters — think about why the sequence still comes out right).
  6. See the division. Swap the button for the function generator (square, 2 Hz, 0–5 V). Scope the clock and Q1: Q1 is a clean square wave at f/4 = 0.5 Hz. Record both frequencies. Counting and dividing: same circuit, two readings.
  7. The scaling question. One sentence for the report: to count 0–15 this way you'd add two more stages — at what cost does each stage's output arrive, and why might a decoder watching all four bits briefly see false codes? (You'll meet the fix in Part 2.)

Part 2 — The 74HC193, Counting Up (≈20 min)

  1. Seat the '193 (16 → +5 V, 8 → GND) and immediately make the three tie-downs: CLR (14) → GND (active HIGH!), LOAD̄ (11) → +5 V, DOWN clk (4) → +5 V (idle clock parks HIGH). Ground the four data inputs (15, 1, 10, 9) for tidiness.
  2. Outputs. Four LEDs, MSB left: QD (7), QC (6), QB (2), QA (3). Note the scattered pin order — copy from Figure 2, not from instinct.
  3. Single-step 0 → 15. Button → UP clk (pin 5). Press sixteen times, recording every fourth state in Data Table 2 (0, 4, 8, 12, 15) and watching the binary count roll. On press sixteen: 1111 → 0000, rollover.
  4. Synchronous proof. Watch the 0111 → 1000 transition closely (three bits fall, one rises — the worst case for a ripple counter). All four LEDs change as one. Contrast with Part 1 in a sentence: where does the '193 put the "which bits toggle?" work?
  5. Test the controls. Mid-count, tap CLR (14) momentarily to +5 V → instant 0000. Then set data inputs to 0101 (A=1, C=1), tap LOAD̄ (11) momentarily to GND → instant 5, and counting resumes from there. Record both in Data Table 2's control rows. Restore ties.

Part 3 — Counting Down (≈15 min)

  1. Swap the clocks. Move the button to DOWN clk (pin 4) and park UP clk (5) → +5 V. (Both clocks must never float.)
  2. Load 15 and descend. Set data inputs 1111, tap LOAD̄ → counter reads 15. Press: 15, 14, 13 … record every third state in Data Table 3 down through 0.
  3. Underflow. At 0000, press once more: 0000 → 1111. While pressing, watch BORROW̄ (pin 13) with a fifth LED or the scope — it pulses LOW exactly at the wrap. Its twin CARRȲ (12) does the same at the top when counting up (check it: swap back briefly). These pulses are the "hand-off" that Part 5's extension uses to cascade digits.
  4. Up/down application. One sentence: name a real system that must count both directions and say which pin its events would drive. (Parking garage: car enters → UP; car exits → DOWN; display shows spaces taken.)

Part 4 — Bending the Modulus: MOD-16 → MOD-10 (≈20 min)

  1. Restore up-counting. Button → UP clk (5), DOWN clk (4) → +5 V, data inputs → GND.
  2. Free CLR from ground — it's about to be driven by logic. Seat the 74HC08 (14/7 power ritual).
  3. Wire the detector per Figure 3: QD (193 pin 7) → 08 pin 1, QB (193 pin 2) → 08 pin 2, and the AND output 08 pin 3 → CLR (193 pin 14). No inverter — the active-HIGH clear meets the gate's HIGH exactly.
  4. Prove MOD-10. Single-step from 0 and record the full sequence in Data Table 4: … 8 (1000), 9 (1001), press → 0 (0000). The count 10 never appears on the LEDs. Explain in one sentence where it went.
  5. Verify the detect logic on paper. In Data Table 4's checklist column, mark for each count 0–9 whether QD·QB = 1. It never is — which is precisely why those two bits alone suffice. What's the first count after 10 that would also trip it? (14 = 1110 — moot, since 10 fires first.)
  6. Design challenge (report). Using the same method, give the detect gate inputs for a MOD-6 counter (0–5, clear at 6 = 0110) — the counter inside every digital clock's minutes column.

Part 5 — Capstone: The Counting Display (≈25 min)

  1. Add the display system. Seat the CD4511 (16 → +5 V, 8 → GND; LT̄ 3 and BL̄ 4 → +5 V, LE 5 → GND) and the 7-segment display (common cathode → GND, one 220 Ω per segment: 4511 pins 13, 12, 11, 10, 9, 15, 14 → segments a–g).
  2. Marry counter to decoder. Four wires: QA (3) → A (7) · QB (2) → B (1) · QC (6) → C (2) · QD (7) → D (6). (Counter pin → 4511 pin; both chips scatter their pins, so check each wire against Figures 2 and 4.)
  3. Single-step the instrument. Press the button ten times and record the displayed digit for each count in Data Table 5: 0, 1, 2 … 9, then 0 again. If any digit renders wrong, the segment map or a BCD wire is crossed — the digit shape tells you which (a missing top bar = segment a).
  4. Set it free. Replace the button with the function generator, 1 Hz. The display counts seconds, 0–9, forever. Sit back for one full cycle — three labs of chips are cooperating in front of you. Then trace one pulse's journey aloud or in writing: edge → count → BCD code → segment pattern → glyph.
  5. Frequency check. Scope QD (or watch its LED): with a 1 Hz clock and MOD-10 counting, QD's cycle repeats every 10 s — f/10, as the cheat sheet promised. Record in Data Table 5.
  6. Extension (describe, optionally build). For 0–99: second '193 + '08 detect + 4511 + display, with CARRȲ… almost. For a decade cascade the tens stage must advance when the ones stage wraps 9 → 0 — your MOD-10 clear pulse marks that moment. Describe the wiring in your report (ones-stage detect output → tens-stage UP clock via an inverter for a clean rising edge), and if time allows, build it and watch 00–99.
  7. Wrap up. Complete the Analysis Questions and Knowledge Check, then Print/Save the report with your data tables.
Troubleshooting
'193 stuck at 0000? CLR (14) is HIGH or floating — it's active HIGH; park it LOW (Parts 2–3) or on the AND output (Parts 4–5). Counter never moves? LOAD̄ (11) must be HIGH and the idle clock pin HIGH, not floating. Counts by 2 or randomly? Clock wired to the wrong pin — UP is 5, DOWN is 4. Display shows 8 constantly? LT̄ (4511 pin 3) is grounded — lamp test. Display blank above 9? Correct behavior if MOD-10 failed and the count reached 10–15: the 4511 blanks invalid BCD — actually a useful diagnostic that your detect gate isn't clearing. Wrong digits (e.g. 2 where 4 should be)? Two BCD wires are swapped — both chips scatter their pins; audit all four against Figure 4's labels.

Every build in this lab, live — including the capstone's 7-segment digit, rendered exactly as the 4511 would drive it. Pulse the clock by hand to single-step, or press Run for a free-running count. The binary LEDs read MSB-left, matching your breadboard convention, and the readout tracks modulus, rollovers, and the detect gate.

What To Notice
In MOD-10 mode, single-step from 8: press once → 9, press again → 0, and the readout flashes the detect gate firing — the count 10 that lived for nanoseconds. In 0–99 Cascade, watch the tens digit: it moves only at the instant the ones digit wraps 9 → 0, exactly one-tenth as often — cascading IS frequency division wearing a display. And compare Ripple and '193 Up at the 7 → 8 transition in the history strip: same arithmetic, different architecture.

✍ Analysis Questions

Answer in complete sentences in your lab report.

  1. Explain why each stage of your Part 1 ripple counter divides frequency by exactly two, tracing the argument back to the T flip-flop's characteristic (two clock edges per Q cycle).
  2. The ripple counter's stages change at slightly different times; the '193's change together. Describe one concrete failure that ripple delay could cause if the Part 1 counter (extended to 4 bits) drove your Part 5 display decoder directly.
  3. The '193's CLR is active HIGH while nearly every control pin you've met (PRE̅, CLR̄, LOAD̄, LT̄, BL̄) is active LOW. What did this asymmetry cost you in wiring habits — and what did it save you in Part 4? State the general lesson about datasheets.
  4. Show why detecting only QD and QB is sufficient for MOD-10: list the binary codes 0–9 and demonstrate that no legal count has both bits high. Then give the detect inputs for MOD-12 (clear at 1100) — the hours counter's cousin.
  5. Your 1 Hz capstone makes QD repeat every 10 s. Design the frequency chain of a digital clock: starting from a 32,768 Hz crystal, list the counter moduli needed to reach 1 Hz, then 1/60 Hz (minutes). (Hint: 32,768 = 2¹⁵.)
  6. In Part 3 the counter underflowed 0 → 15. In unsigned binary arithmetic this same wraparound produces famous software bugs (integer overflow). Give one example of a real-world overflow failure and state, in counter terms, what the software forgot to check.
  7. Security connection: Trusted Platform Modules and secure enclaves contain monotonic counters — hardware counters that can only count up, never down, never reset by software. Explain (a) how a monotonic counter defeats a replay attack in which an adversary re-submits an old, validly-signed message or rolls back firmware to a vulnerable version, and (b) which two pins of your 74HC193 would have to be physically disabled to approximate one. What does it say about hardware security that the protection is a missing capability?
